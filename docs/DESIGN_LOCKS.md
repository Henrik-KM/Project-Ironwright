# Project Ironwight — Design Locks

**Status:** Canonical product contract  
**Purpose:** Prevent implementation drift back toward conventional RTS, tower-defence, automation, or colony-management design.

These rules override older Project Ironwight material. A proposed feature that conflicts with this file should be rejected or escalated as a deliberate product-direction change.

## 1. The game is survival strategy, not an RTS

The player directly inhabits the Mechromancer and protects one home in a hostile world. Strategy comes from dangerous commitments, irreversible or costly evolution choices, and deciding when to leave, intervene, sacrifice, or retreat.

Strategy must not be manufactured by giving the player many simultaneous administrative systems.

## 2. One home

There is one primary base: the original Heartforge.

The Heartforge can grow, fold outward, reinforce itself, and develop a limited defensive envelope. It does not become a network of colonies, sectors, production sites, or claimed territory.

Robot excursions may visit distant locations, recover objects, scout, hunt, or complete objectives. They do not establish a permanent territorial economy.

## 3. Base building means base evolution

The player chooses what the Heartforge should become. Machines handle exact placement, construction sequence, repair, rebuilding, and local adaptation.

Manual placement may appear briefly in a greybox prototype or as an emergency exception, but it must not become the optimal or expected long-term interaction model.

The player should never be required to place repeated wall segments, power poles, storage crates, repair stations, or identical turrets.

## 4. One ordinary resource

The game may have at most one ordinary stockpiled construction resource: **Scrap**.

Rare components, biological samples, schematics, memories, or cores are unique progression objects. They are not parallel currencies with recurring income rates.

Do not add separate metal, crystal, fuel, energy, food, ammunition, electronics, alloy, science, population, or command-point economies.

Power may exist fictionally and visually, but it is not a player-managed network or recurring spreadsheet.

## 5. Autonomy removes work

As the machine population grows, the number of routine player decisions must stay flat or decline.

Progression should permanently remove categories of supervision:

- routine gathering;
- routine repair;
- replacement of ordinary losses;
- base upkeep;
- exact defensive positioning;
- local target choice;
- expedition composition and routing;
- ordinary threat response.

An autonomy upgrade that merely opens a more complex command interface is not acceptable.

## 6. No unit micro-management

The game does not require routine selection or issuing orders to individual robots.

There are no mandatory per-unit loadouts, skill bars, ammunition inventories, patrol routes, worker assignments, production queues, or formation controls.

Early interaction with one robot may be intimate and direct. That relationship must not scale into managing a hundred individual units.

## 7. No scheduled-wave main loop

The world applies continuous, ecological pressure. Enemies move, feed, scout, reproduce, stalk, infest, migrate, and respond to disturbance.

There is no recurring countdown such as “Wave 17 in five minutes.” Large attacks are rare consequences of world state or player action: disturbing a colony, activating a detectable system, provoking an apex creature, or beginning the endgame.

Continuous pressure does not mean uninterrupted combat. Routine pressure should often be handled automatically and expressed through wear, losses, sounds, tracks, and changing risk.

## 8. Enemies are organic

The hostile world is biological or bio-alien: chitin, muscle, bone, membranes, burrows, spores, nests, parasites, pack behaviour, territorial behaviour, and apex predation.

Hostile robots, robot armies, corrupted machines, and mirror-machine factions are outside the core direction.

Machines belong to the player fantasy. The contrast between created order and hostile life is visually and mechanically important.

## 9. The opening is small and frightening

The first hours must not show a functioning fortress, large map, broad command interface, or confident army.

The player begins with:

- a damaged Heartforge;
- one primitive or damaged robot;
- a weak tool or weapon;
- little Scrap;
- a tiny reliable sensor radius;
- no complete perimeter;
- darkness containing threats the player cannot safely fight.

Initial excursions are measured in tens of metres, not map regions.

## 10. One long sandbox mode

The principal product is one persistent sandbox run, saved and continued over many sessions.

A successful run may take 30–100 hours. The exact duration is a balance target, not a timer. Players are expected to lose several worlds before achieving the first victory.

Do not redesign the main game around 45–90 minute runs, short scenario campaigns, or repeated wave maps.

## 11. The base remains under pressure

The Heartforge is never permanently solved. The outside world remains dangerous, local conditions change, and growth exposes the base to stronger attention.

This does not justify chores. The player should confront strategic vulnerability, not maintenance busywork.

## 12. No empire layer

Do not add:

- territory claiming;
- forward bases or permanent outposts;
- supply lines between owned sites;
- map-painting;
- economic sectors;
- regional tax, population, or workforce management;
- multiple production centres;
- grand-strategy diplomacy.

Distant activity is expeditionary and temporary.

## 13. Limited choices, high consequence

The game should present relatively few decisions, each with meaningful consequences.

Good decisions:

- whether to risk an excursion now;
- whether the Mechromancer or machines should go;
- which major evolution to choose;
- whether to save trapped machines or protect the Heartforge;
- whether to provoke a dangerous organism for a critical component;
- when to initiate the final process.

Bad decisions:

- which of twelve storage buildings to upgrade;
- whether turret 37 needs ammunition;
- how many workers should repair the west wall;
- which five robots should use a slightly different gun;
- which resource route needs another hauler.

## 14. Anti-chore acceptance test

Every feature must answer these questions:

1. Does it create a new recurring task?
2. Does that task become more frequent as the base grows?
3. Can machines solve it automatically after the player has understood it once?
4. Is the decision strategically meaningful, or merely operational upkeep?
5. Could the same tension be produced with one aggregated choice?

A feature fails the product contract if player workload scales roughly with the number of buildings, robots, attacks, objectives, or hours played.

## 15. No survival-meter bureaucracy

The survival feeling comes from vulnerability, darkness, distance from safety, injury, hostile creatures, and costly risk—not from maintaining hunger, thirst, sleep, temperature, and hygiene bars.

A limited health/integrity system is necessary. Routine bodily-maintenance meters are not.

## 16. UI becomes calmer as autonomy improves

Early UI can be immediate and personal. Later UI should summarise machine intent and surface only exceptions that require strategic input.

The late game must not become a dashboard of dozens of rates, alerts, and production graphs.

## 17. Art must support the product

Early imagery is intimate, dark, sparse, and low-tech. Mid-game imagery shows a compact self-maintaining defence. Late imagery shows density, coordination, and scale around the same Heartforge.

The concept art is not permission to create a sprawling RTS base or to treat every shown UI element as a required system.

## 18. Definition of success

The game succeeds when a player can describe this transformation:

> “At first I was afraid to walk beyond the Heartforge’s light with one useless machine beside me. Much later, the machines were defending the same home and returning from expeditions without needing me to tell each one what to do.”

If the player instead says, “My base got large and I spent most of the time servicing it,” the design has failed.
