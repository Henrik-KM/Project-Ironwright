# Project Ironwright — Design Locks

**Status:** Canonical product contract  
**Authority:** The current Project Ironwright conversation with Henrik overrides older repository text when an explicit instruction conflicts with it.  
**Purpose:** Prevent implementation drift toward a chore-heavy RTS, tower-defence game, or colony-management simulator while preserving the current autonomous-outpost direction.

## 1. Survival strategy, not an RTS

The player directly inhabits the Mechromancer and protects one run-critical Heartforge in a hostile world. Strategy comes from dangerous commitments, costly evolution choices, machine priorities, and deciding when to leave, intervene, sacrifice, or retreat.

Strategy must not be manufactured by giving the player many simultaneous administrative systems.

## 2. One primary home

The original Heartforge is the only player-operated home, fabrication centre, and run-critical base. If it is destroyed without a later continuity technology, the run ends.

The Heartforge grows through tiers. Higher tiers unlock stronger machines, deeper autonomy, adaptive base construction, and a bounded network of autonomous outposts.

Outposts are not replacement bases. The player cannot relocate to them, fabricate a parallel settlement there, or manually manage workers, power, queues, inventories, or layouts.

## 3. Base building means intent and evolution

The player chooses what the Heartforge and machine society should become. Machines handle exact placement, construction sequence, routine repair, rebuilding, and local adaptation.

Manual placement may appear only as an emergency exception or development aid. It must never become the optimal long-term interaction model.

The player should not place repeated wall segments, power poles, storage crates, repair stations, identical turrets, or individual outpost modules.

## 4. Autonomous outposts are canonical

A Heartforge tier upgrade may unlock outpost projects at fixed sites that were first discovered through real excursions.

An outpost project follows these rules:

- the player chooses a discovered site and a strategic purpose;
- the player does not place the structure or select individual builders;
- an Engineer group and escorts physically leave the Heartforge and travel through the persistent world;
- the group maintains cohesion, constructs the outpost, and physically returns;
- the outpost automatically performs its role and requests no routine input;
- routine repair happens automatically using Scrap;
- a destroyed outpost is automatically rebuilt when robots and Scrap are available;
- resource output is physically collected by robot groups rather than teleported home;
- the player may inspect and follow operations but not micro-manage them.

Valid purposes include resource gathering, proxy defence, early warning/scouting, and field repair for remote robots.

The number of sites is bounded. Outposts do not create territory ownership, map painting, population, workforce assignment, manual supply routes, or a second production economy.

## 5. One ordinary resource

The game may have at most one ordinary stockpiled construction resource: **Scrap**.

Rare components, biological samples, schematics, memories, cores, and similar progression objects are discrete discoveries. They are not parallel recurring currencies with separate income dashboards.

Do not add separate metal, crystal, fuel, energy, food, ammunition, electronics, alloy, science, population, or command-point economies.

Power may exist fictionally and visually, but it is not a player-managed network or recurring spreadsheet.

## 6. Autonomy removes work

As the machine population, base, and outpost network grow, the number of routine player decisions must stay flat or decline.

Progression should permanently remove categories of supervision:

- routine gathering;
- routine repair;
- replacement of ordinary losses;
- base and outpost upkeep;
- exact defensive positioning;
- local target choice;
- expedition, construction, and haul composition;
- route selection;
- ordinary threat response;
- resource hauling;
- rebuilding destroyed outposts.

An autonomy upgrade that merely opens a more complex command interface is not acceptable.

## 7. No unit micro-management

The game does not require routine selection or orders to individual robots.

There are no mandatory per-unit loadouts, skill bars, ammunition inventories, patrol routes, worker assignments, production queues, formation controls, or manually assembled construction teams.

Early interaction with one companion robot may be intimate and direct. That relationship must not scale into managing a hundred individual units.

## 8. Robots perform the recurring loop

The Mechromancer initially demonstrates salvage and manually fabricates the first machines at the Heartforge. The long-term loop then shifts to robots:

- coordinated salvage groups leave and return;
- expeditions physically cross the world;
- Engineers and escorts construct and upgrade outposts;
- autonomous groups haul resources;
- machines defend, repair, rebuild, scout, and replace routine losses.

The player selects high-level focus, technologies, discovered sites, strategic purposes, acceptable risk, and major operations—not individual routes and tasks.

## 9. No scheduled-wave main loop

The world applies continuous ecological pressure. Enemies move, feed, scout, reproduce, stalk, infest, migrate, and respond to disturbance.

There is no recurring countdown such as “Wave 17 in five minutes.” Large attacks are rare consequences of world state or player action: loud salvage, outpost construction, disturbing a colony, activating a detectable system, provoking an apex creature, or beginning the endgame.

Continuous pressure does not mean uninterrupted combat. Routine pressure should often be handled automatically and expressed through wear, losses, sounds, tracks, changing risk, and machine reports.

## 10. Enemies are organic

The hostile world is biological or bio-alien: chitin, muscle, bone, membranes, burrows, spores, nests, parasites, pack behaviour, territorial behaviour, migration, and apex predation.

Hostile robots, corrupted machines, and mirror-machine armies are outside the normal enemy fantasy.

Machines belong to the player. The contrast between created order and hostile life is visually and mechanically important.

## 11. The opening is small, weak, and frightening

The first hours must not show a functioning fortress, broad command interface, confident army, or available outpost network.

The player begins with:

- a damaged Heartforge;
- one indispensable companion robot;
- a weak automatic pistol that cannot carry sustained combat;
- little Scrap;
- a tiny reliable sensor radius;
- no complete perimeter;
- darkness and ruined streets containing threats the player cannot safely fight.

Manual salvage and early robot fabrication take time, emit noise, disable attack, and expose the Mechromancer. Initial excursions are measured in tens of metres.

The world should be oppressive but readable. The Heartforge is warm, cozy, and inhabited; the surrounding town is cold, beautiful, and dangerous.

## 12. The full world always exists

Remote machines, enemies, outposts, wrecks, sites, and objectives retain physical positions and continue to exist in the same simulation.

An expedition, construction team, rebuild convoy, or haul group is not a detached timer or probability roll. The player can follow its path, observe encounters, and find disabled machines at the locations where they failed.

Reduced-detail simulation may be used for performance, but it must preserve equivalent state, causality, and outcomes.

## 13. Coordinated groups

Remote groups use leaders, formation slots, cohesion rules, escort roles, regrouping, retreat logic, and shared pace.

The default behaviour is not “every robot runs independently at maximum speed.” Groups slow for vulnerable or damaged members and stop to regroup when cohesion breaks.

Faster, looser, or sacrificial movement may be a deliberate later doctrine with explicit trade-offs.

## 14. One long sandbox mode

The principal product is one persistent sandbox run saved and continued over many sessions.

A successful run may take roughly 30–100 hours. The exact duration is a balance target, not a timer. Players are expected to lose several worlds before achieving the first victory.

Do not redesign the main product around 45–90 minute runs, repeated wave maps, or a short-run roguelite loop.

## 15. Base defence remains primary

The Heartforge is never permanently solved. The outside world remains dangerous, local conditions change, and growth exposes the base and remote installations to stronger attention.

Outposts can screen routes, gather material, warn of danger, and repair machines, but cannot remove the Heartforge’s central vulnerability.

This does not justify chores. The player confronts strategic risk, not maintenance busywork.

## 16. Slow, constrained expansion

The base grows slowly and remains compact relative to the world. Robots travel outward to gather, scout, hunt, recover unique objects, construct bounded support posts, and complete objectives.

Expansion is not land claiming. The player does not paint the map, establish continuous borders, or convert the hostile world into a conventional empire.

## 17. Limited choices, high consequence

Good decisions include:

- whether to risk loud salvage now;
- whether the Mechromancer or machines should undertake an objective;
- which major evolution or robot family to prioritize;
- which discovered site and outpost purpose best addresses the current weakness;
- whether to save trapped machines or protect the Heartforge;
- whether to provoke a dangerous organism for a critical component;
- when to initiate an irreversible late-game process.

Bad decisions include:

- which of twelve storage buildings to upgrade;
- whether turret 37 needs ammunition;
- how many workers repair the west wall;
- which five robots use a slightly different gun;
- which route needs another hauler;
- which individual robot should rebuild an outpost.

## 18. Failure must be legible

Long-run failure is expected, but collapse must have understandable causes.

The game should retain enough event history to explain:

- which constraint failed first;
- what threat or decision created the decisive pressure;
- whether expansion exceeded machine capacity;
- whether a doctrine, technology choice, or neglected warning contributed;
- which recovery options remained available.

Persistent between-run progression should mostly preserve knowledge, discoveries, and understanding rather than simply grant overwhelming permanent statistics.

## 19. Anti-chore acceptance test

Every feature must answer:

1. Does it create a new recurring task?
2. Does that task become more frequent as the base, machine count, or outpost count grows?
3. Can machines solve it automatically after the player has understood it once?
4. Is the decision strategically meaningful, or merely operational upkeep?
5. Could the same tension be produced with one aggregated choice?

A feature fails the product contract if player workload scales roughly with buildings, robots, outposts, attacks, objectives, or hours played.

## 20. No survival-meter bureaucracy

The survival feeling comes from vulnerability, darkness, distance from safety, injury, hostile creatures, noisy commitments, and costly risk—not hunger, thirst, sleep, temperature, and hygiene bars.

A limited health/integrity system is necessary. Routine bodily-maintenance meters are not.

## 21. UI becomes calmer as autonomy improves

Early UI can be immediate and personal. Later UI should summarize machine intent and surface only exceptions requiring strategic input.

The outpost interface exposes site, purpose, tier, broad status, and authorization. It must not become a dashboard of workers, queues, throughput, budgets, ammunition, or repair assignments.

## 22. Art supports the transformation

Early imagery is intimate, grounded, urban, warm near the Heartforge, and frightening outside it. Mid-game imagery shows a compact self-maintaining defence and a few vulnerable support nodes. Late imagery shows density, coordination, and increasingly futuristic machines growing out of the same ruined town.

Concept art is not permission to create a sprawling RTS base or to treat every shown UI element as a required system.

## 23. Definition of success

The game succeeds when a player can describe this transformation:

> “At first I was afraid to leave the Heartforge light and depended on one machine to survive. Much later, coordinated robots were salvaging, defending, building and rebuilding remote support posts, and returning from deep expeditions without me telling each one what to do.”

If the player instead says, “My base got large and I spent most of the time servicing it,” the design has failed.
